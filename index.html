Building the AI Classroom

This resource page provides strategies for 1) designing an AI course in social work; 2) aligning courses with the Council on Social Work Education's 2029 Educational Policy and Accreditation Standards (EPAS); 3) developing classroom and PhD program AI policies informed by the ETHICS framework; and 4) finding other related sources.

Theme 1

Designing an AI course in social work

Questions to consider

  1. Does your university already provide AI literacy training? If so, what content is covered and what content is left out?
  2. What is the minimum level of discipline-specific (social work) AI literacy your students need, and what are those domains?
  3. How might you ensure the course is in alignment with CSWE's competencies?
  4. Which assignments might work best to teach not only AI use but critical analysis of AI use, outputs, and impacts?

Five strategies for developing an AI course in a social work program

  1. Build the course as a tiered progression from literacy to competency. Following Yang and Liechty's (2026) three-tier model, Tier 1 gives every MSW student foundational AI literacy, Tier 2 builds applied, role-specific skills, and Tier 3 develops leadership, governance, and research capacity for doctoral students.
  2. Tie every module and assessment to a named EPAS 2022 competency. Each assignment should link to one or two competencies. Assessments should move from what students know, to how they can show critical analysis, and to what they do in field placements.
  3. Grade critical thinking and human judgment, not polished output. AI tools invite over-reliance and skill loss, especially among less experienced workers, including most MSW students. Assignments should make the reasoning and critical analysis visible. For example, students write their own assessment first, then have an AI challenge it, document what changed, and keep a detailed log of their AI use.
  4. Teach ethics, equity, and data governance in every unit, not in a single week. Each module should ask who benefits, who is burdened, what consent is required, and what law applies, drawing on HIPAA, FERPA, and NASW Code standards.
Theme 2

2029 EPAS alignment

CSWE's 2025 Technology Task Force recommended making technology, including AI, prominent in the 2029 EPAS. Draft 1 of the 2029 EPAS was released in October 2026 and is now open for public comment. Draft 1 threads AI through existing competencies and proposes a new one (Competency 10: Ethical and Effective Use of Technology in Practice). Assuming the AI language and the new competency are retained in the final version, here are some things to keep in mind.

Where AI shows up in Draft 1

CompetencyAI and technology emphasis
C1 EthicsUse emerging technologies and AI ethically, and understand how they shape the practice environment.
C5 PolicyAnalyze how AI and digital-system policies affect privacy, self-determination, and access to services.
C9 EvaluationRecognize the opportunities and limits of data, AI, and digital systems.
C10 TechnologyNew: evaluate, select, and integrate technology, including AI, and examine its effect on access to resources.

Paraphrased from the CSWE 2029 EPAS Draft 1 page (Oct 2026). Check the draft for exact wording before using it in course documents.

Questions to consider

  1. Does anything surprise you about how AI is mentioned in the 2029 EPAS?
  2. Given the 2029 EPAS, what in your course stays the same, and what needs to change?
  3. How have you assessed or would assess AI-related learning in your course (e.g., simulations, other assignments involving AI)?

Theme 3

AI policy

Questions to consider

  1. Is your current syllabus policy about permission (allowed or banned) or about practice (how to use AI well)?
  2. Should a PhD program policy differ from a course policy? Think about comprehensive exams, dissertations, and AI as a research method versus a writing aid.
  3. How do you handle field: agency rules, client data, and AI documentation tools students already see at placement?

Applying the ETHICS framework

The ETHICS framework (Lee et al., 2025, JSSWR) sets six principles for AI use in social work research. The same principles give a structure for course syllabi and doctoral program guidance, where students are both learners and emerging researchers.

PrincipleAI policy
EEquityAI policy should ensure access to approved tools so policy does not reward students who can pay for premium AI.
TTransparencyAI policy should require a short AI-use statement on assignments: tool, purpose, and what the student changed.
HHuman-centerednessAI policy needs to note that the student is accountable for every claim and citation and should verify all AI output.
IIntegrityAI policy should encourage ongoing learning and the development of AI knowledge and competency.
CCollaborationAI policy could emphasize students being open and curious while also critical of AI outputs in collaborative projects.
SSafetyAI policy should clearly name stop points, such as no AI with case notes or identifiable data.

Example policies

Course syllabus Ohio State College of Social Work: Course Policy AI Statement Permitted uses, academic integrity, and a four-part AI disclosure with an example. Click to read the full statement.

The Ohio State University College of Social Work promotes the development of technology literacy for all students and faculty. Accordingly, the College of Social Work allows students to use artificial intelligence (AI) tools in limited ways that support their learning.

Generally permitted uses of AI tools

Students may use AI tools for the following purposes:

  • Writing assistance: Improve spelling, grammar, and the structure of your own writing.
  • Idea development: Brainstorming and initial idea generation.

When using AI tools beyond these generally permitted uses, students must follow all course assignments and instructor guidelines, including limitations placed on the use of AI tools.

Academic integrity and AI

While AI can be a valuable tool, remember that academic integrity remains paramount. Attribute AI-generated content with proper citations and avoid plagiarism. Additionally, consider the accuracy of information incorporated in your assignment and the ethical implications of using AI in educational contexts.

Disclosing your use of AI tools

If using AI tools, briefly include the following information with your submission, either at the end of your assignment on a new page or in an additional attachment. This should be brief, but enough to explain your usage:

  • Application used: Specify the AI application or tool you used (e.g., Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude AI, Gemini).
  • Purpose: Describe the purpose for which you used AI (e.g., idea generation, writing assistance, content creation).
  • Iteration and refinement: Briefly explain the quality of the initial output and how you revised prompts or adjusted parameters to refine the AI output. Did you experiment with different input prompts to improve the results?
  • Incorporation in completed assignment: Briefly reflect on how you incorporated the AI-generated content into your assignment. How did you edit, adapt, or combine it with other ideas?
Example disclosure: For the engaging a group assignment, I used ChatGPT to brainstorm different group topics and structures to familiarize myself with how groups are usually structured. I then asked for examples of groups, which I used to conduct further research through the library. Initial responses were very generic, but I used follow-up questions to probe and get more specific ideas. My group is based on one of the ideas. I also used Grammarly for writing assistance and feedback.

PhD program Ohio State College of Social Work PhD Program: Guidelines on the Use of AI in Candidacy and Dissertation Expectations, appropriate and inappropriate uses, and sample disclosure statements for candidacy exams and dissertations. Click to read a summary.

PhD students may use AI-assisted tools that align with three expectations: submissions are the student's original scholarship and writing; students demonstrate mastery of skills independent of AI; and the work is authentically human, with the student as the driver of the scholarship. Students consult their advisor and committee before using AI, verify all AI-generated information, and refer to Ohio State's approved AI tools and the ETHICS framework. A detailed disclosure statement is required whenever AI is used.

Candidacy examination

Appropriate

  • Writing assistance: spelling, grammar, and structure.
  • Supporting academic and non-academic literature searches, with references verified by the student.

Inappropriate

  • Copying and pasting AI-generated text into candidacy documents.
  • Using AI to brainstorm ideas or draft research questions that do not originate from the student.
  • Using AI to synthesize information, draw conclusions, or generate content.
  • Uploading candidacy questions to AI for guidance during the exam period.

Dissertation

Appropriate

  • Writing assistance: spelling, grammar, and structure.
  • Data preparation, such as transcription the student checks against the audio.
  • Data analysis, when appropriate and in consultation with the advisor and committee.

Inappropriate

  • Copying and pasting AI-generated text into the dissertation.
  • Using AI to brainstorm ideas or draft research questions that do not originate from the student.
  • Using AI to summarize and synthesize findings, draw conclusions, or generate content.
Example disclosure (candidacy): I am the sole author of this candidacy paper. Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools were used exclusively for language proofreading (e.g., breaking down long sentences) and for supporting both academic and non-academic literature searches. All suggested language changes and identified literature were reviewed and verified by me to ensure accuracy. The structural organization, original (non-referenced) content, argumentation, proposed methods, and conclusions presented in this candidacy submission are entirely my own work.

Submissions substantially developed by AI, or containing sections substantially developed by AI, are not accepted. Suspected misuse may be referred to the Committee on Academic Misconduct, with candidacy or progress toward graduation paused, and allegations of AI-enabled plagiarism, fabrication, or falsification are reviewed under the University Policy on Research Misconduct.
